Why print colours look different from your screen

Published by WallMock · Updated 31 October 2026

Short answer: A screen makes colour with light; a print reflects light from ink and paper or canvas. They cannot be expected to look identical in every room or on every device. Use the file settings your print provider specifies, check bright and deep colours carefully, and order a physical sample before making a colour-sensitive promise to buyers.

A print-on-demand room mockup helps buyers picture the artwork on a wall. It is not a colour proof of the print. Keep those jobs separate: use a screen image to show the design and room, then check the provider’s print file and a real sample for the physical result.

Why the same artwork can look different

A monitor emits light. A print is seen by light reflecting from its surface. Screen brightness, colour settings, room lighting and the print material all affect what you see. Very saturated RGB colours may not have an exact printable equivalent; Printful notes that some bright colours can print less vividly than they appear on a monitor (Printful file-preparation guidance). That is a limit of comparing two different media, not a reason to keep pushing saturation until a phone preview looks brighter.

There is no single screen appearance that every buyer will see. A bright phone in a dark room, a laptop with a warm display setting and a print beside a window are three different viewing conditions. Treat an on-screen comparison as a check for obvious shifts, not a calibrated proof of the print.

Start with the provider’s file instructions

Check the product page for the exact product you sell. Providers can specify different colour profiles, file types, resolution and print processes. For Printful, the current guidance recommends sRGB IEC61966-2.1 for its uploaded print files and describes how its production system converts those colours (Printful’s RGB and CMYK guidance). Follow your own provider’s instructions if they differ; do not convert a file to CMYK just because someone says all print files must be CMYK.

Keep the print file separate from listing images. Export a clean artwork file to the provider’s requirements. Export a smaller, correctly framed listing image for the shop. A mockup export is a picture of a product in a room; it does not replace the production artwork file.

If you use Printify, its Product Creator has RGB and CMYK mockup preview modes. Printify says the selected preview mode also affects mockups uploaded to the sales channel, while the original uploaded design file is not changed (Printify’s mockup colour-mode explanation). Check the setting before exporting those listing photos.

A fair screen-to-print check

  1. Open the original artwork and the listing image on the same display, at a comfortable brightness. Turn off temporary warm-night display settings for the comparison.
  2. Compare the same small area in each: a pale neutral, a deep shadow and the strongest saturated colour. Do not judge colour from an entire room scene with warm lamps and coloured walls.
  3. Look at the file’s colour profile and provider requirements before changing anything. If a profile is missing or the file has been converted, solve that first.
  4. For a sale-critical colour, order the exact product and finish you plan to sell. View the sample in ordinary daylight, away from direct sun, and note what changes.

Keep the original file unchanged. If the sample is darker, duller or warmer than expected, compare it with the provider’s current guidance and adjust the production file deliberately. Do not edit the mockup to imply that the physical print is brighter than it is.

Use precise captions: “room mockup” for a staged scene and “sample photo” only for the physical item shown. Etsy says listing photos must accurately represent what buyers receive. It requires an unedited photo of the actual item for most listings, with exceptions for some item types; check the rule that applies to your product (Etsy’s listing image requirements).

A worked example: orange, rust and olive

Imagine an abstract with a bright orange shape, a muted rust field and an olive accent. The room mockup can show the art above a sofa and make the overall palette easy to understand. It cannot tell you whether the orange ink on a particular matte canvas will look as vivid as the orange pixels on your laptop.

  • Confirm the print file uses the profile and export settings requested by the provider.
  • Check the orange and olive in a provider preview if one is available, while remembering that a preview is still on a screen.
  • Order a sample in the product and finish you intend to list.
  • Photograph the sample honestly, and caption the room mockup and sample photo as different things.

For a step-by-step physical comparison, use the sample check against your mockup. Use the print resolution checker for pixel dimensions; colour and resolution are separate checks.

Questions

Will my wall art print look exactly like it does on my phone?

No screen can promise an exact match to ink on a physical surface in every viewing condition. Use your provider's file requirements and check a physical sample for the product you plan to sell.

Should I convert every artwork file to CMYK?

No. Use the colour profile specified by your print provider. For example, Printful currently recommends sRGB IEC61966-2.1 for uploaded print files; another provider may give different instructions.

Is a room mockup a colour proof?

No. A room mockup shows how the artwork and product could look in a room. It does not prove the exact printed colour, paper, canvas or finish.
