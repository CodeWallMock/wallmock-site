Wall art placement in mockups: size, balance and usable wall space

Published by WallMock · Updated 28 October 2026

Short answer: Find the uninterrupted wall that can hold the whole framed piece, then centre the art on its visual anchor, such as a sofa, sideboard or chair, rather than on the photo. Start generous, check clearance from lamps, doors and ceilings, keep the artwork's own ratio, match perspective and light on angled walls, and check the result at thumbnail size.

Choose a convincing art position by reading the furniture, architecture and empty wall, not just centring the entire image. Every room in WallMock's library of mockup rooms is reviewed for the products and shapes it can show.

Find the usable wall first

A room image has a centre, but that is not always the centre of the wall where art belongs. Identify doorways, glazing, wall returns, moulding, lamps and furniture before placing a picture. Mark the uninterrupted wall area that can hold the entire product, including the frame. A wide photograph may contain only a narrow useful hanging area.

Choose the visual anchor

Above a sideboard, the sideboard can be a stronger visual anchor than the photograph’s midpoint. In a reading corner, a chair and its surrounding wall may form the relevant composition. On a plain gallery wall, the open wall itself may be the anchor. Decide which relationship you want the viewer to notice, then centre the artwork or group within that relationship.

Make the artwork prominent without crowding it

Start with a generous picture size (our guide to how high and how wide to hang art over furniture has the proportions), then check clearance from furniture, ceiling details and nearby objects. If a frame nearly touches a lamp or doorway in the image, the composition can feel accidental. Shrinking everything is not always the answer: another room may suit the product better. A landscape over a broad sideboard and a portrait in a tall niche solve different spatial problems.

Treat ratios as room-specific choices

A 3:1 panorama needs horizontal space. A 1:3 piece needs a tall uninterrupted area (see mocking up panoramic and tall art). Neither rule tells you the correct position or size by itself. A square may look balanced in one room and lost in another. Keep the original artwork proportions and choose a scene that accommodates them; do not stretch the artwork to make it fit.

Check perspective and lighting together

A frontal room is easier to judge because the wall and artwork edges are close to parallel. An angled wall needs matching perspective, believable visible frame depth and a shadow that follows the same light direction. A dramatic sunbeam across the room should not leave the mounted object looking unrelated to its surroundings. Inspect both the complete image and the frame corners.

Review the image at the size buyers see

Reduce the mockup to a small listing preview. Can you recognise the artwork immediately? Is a large plant or decorative object taking attention away from it? Then inspect the export at full size for clipping and edge problems. Room staging is a visual presentation, not a calibrated measuring tool: do not infer exact physical hanging dimensions from furniture unless the scene has reliable scale information. In WallMock, use the room’s supported products and ratios, and review each result before publishing.

Questions

Where should art go in a mockup?

On the uninterrupted wall that can hold the whole framed piece, centred on its visual anchor, such as the sofa, sideboard or chair below it.

Should art be centred in the photo?

Not necessarily. The centre of the photo is often not the centre of the usable wall; centre the art on the furniture or wall area it belongs to.

Can a mockup show exact hanging dimensions?

Only if the scene has reliable scale information. Room staging is a visual presentation, not a calibrated measuring tool.
