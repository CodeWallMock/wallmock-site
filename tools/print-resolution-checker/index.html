How the checker works

DPI (dots per inch) is how many of your file's pixels land on each inch of the print. The checker fills each print size edge to edge with no border, crops what doesn't fit, and reports the DPI of the side that limits the size. That is why a 4,000 × 6,000 px file shows 250 DPI and 16.7% cropped at 16 × 20: the 4,000 px side covers 16 in, and part of the long side is cut to make the 4:5 shape.

  • Excellent: 300 DPI or more.
  • Good: 200–299 DPI.
  • Fair: 150–199 DPI, fine for large prints seen from a distance.
  • Low: under 150 DPI; the print may look soft.

Your print provider may set its own minimum; check their file guidelines before you list a size. To see which sizes your file fits without any cropping, use the print size calculator.

Three resolution mistakes to avoid

  • Typing 300 into the DPI box. Changing the DPI setting without resampling adds no pixels. A 2,400 × 3,000 px file prints 8 × 10 in at 300 DPI whatever its settings say.
  • Checking one side only. When the print is a different shape from the file, one side sets the DPI and the other is cropped; the lower of the two DPI figures is the real one. Read the checker's Cropped column as well as its DPI.
  • Listing sizes the file can't carry. A 4,000 × 6,000 px file gives 167 DPI at 24 × 36 in. Fine for a large print seen across a room, soft up close. If a size you sell falls under 150 DPI, upscale the master before printing or drop the size.

Before I sell a file at a size

My print masters are upscaled before they go to print, and every image is checked at 100% before upload: corners, edges, signatures, small marks. Nothing with a known defect is shown. A DPI number only tells you a size is possible. The 100% view tells you whether the file is clean enough to sell at that size.

Questions

What resolution do I need to print 24 × 36 inches?

7,200 × 10,800 pixels for 300 DPI. Large posters seen from a distance often look fine at 150–200 DPI, which is 3,600 × 5,400 to 4,800 × 7,200 pixels.

What DPI should art prints be?

300 DPI is the usual target for fine-art prints viewed up close. 200 DPI is often fine for medium prints, and 150 DPI for large posters seen from across a room.

How do I find my file's pixel size?

Choose your file in the checker above; it reads the width and height in your browser. You can also type the numbers in.

Why does my art crop on some sizes?

Every print size has a shape. A 2:3 file fits 8 × 12, 12 × 18 and 24 × 36 exactly, but an 8 × 10 print is 4:5, so part of a 2:3 file is cut off.

Does changing the DPI setting make my file print bigger?

No. Changing the DPI number without resampling keeps every pixel, so the sharp print size stays the same: 2,400 × 3,000 px is 8 × 10 in at 300 DPI whatever the file's settings say. Only more pixels make a bigger sharp print.

Is 150 DPI good enough for a print?

For a large print seen from across a room, usually; the checker rates 150–199 DPI as fair. For a small print people look at from arm's length, aim for 300 DPI. Below 150 DPI the print may look soft.

Is my file uploaded?

No. The checker reads only the image's size in your browser. The file never leaves your device.
